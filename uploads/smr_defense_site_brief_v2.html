Design brief — SMR Defense website v2

Revised: SMR standalone, claims kept safe

This is a self-contained brief for designing the SMR Defense public-facing site. Two hard constraints drive every decision in this version: (1) SMR Defense is presented as its own entity — no ecosystem parent, no distribution relationships named; (2) no specific claim appears on the site that could become inaccurate, change, create liability, or reveal internal strategy to competitors.

What's been removed from v1 and why
GridMarket — internal relationship, not public positioning. Specific pricing ($250K/yr) — subject to change, creates anchor problems. "NERC CIP compliant" as a badge — compliance status is aspirational / in-progress, claiming it publicly is risky. "No Chinese hardware" as an explicit statement — current hardware mix may include Chinese-origin components; this could create legal exposure. Named partners (Xerox, OEM names) — partnership terms may be confidential or not yet formalized for public announcement. Specific site counts or pipeline figures — these belong to other entities and change.

Part 1b — Universal trust signals

What every visitor needs in the first 10 seconds

The site's only job in the first scroll: establish that SMR Defense is a serious company solving a real and urgent problem in a market that is structurally underserved. Everything else is elaboration.

  • The problem is real and now — AI power demand, aging infrastructure, and new compliance pressure have created a gap
  • SMR Defense is built specifically for regulated infrastructure environments — not retrofitted from consumer robotics
  • The team has firsthand experience in high-security, high-stakes physical operations
  • This is a service model, not a hardware sale — ongoing relationship, not a one-time transaction
  • There is a clear, fast path to a real conversation — not a wait-list or a form that goes nowhere
Part 2 — Design vision

Look, feel, and tone direction

Aesthetic reference

Palantir, Anduril, a serious infrastructure fund's investor page. Not a startup. Not a drone company. The brand should feel like it belongs in a room with utilities executives and defense contractors — earned gravity, not performed toughness.

Color

Near-black primary surfaces. Brand teal from the existing wolf-shield logo as the sole accent — used only for CTAs, key label badges, and active states. White type throughout. No gradients, no glow, no color noise.

Typography

Geometric sans for body and UI. A condensed display weight for hero numbers and section openers — command and control feel. Tight tracking on category labels. Two weights only.

Imagery

Real quadruped/drone in real infrastructure environments if available. Otherwise: dark substation photography, data center corridors, power infrastructure. No renders, no stock smiles, no abstract blobs of light.

Write like thisBuilt for the environments where failure isn't an option.
Not like thisWe're reimagining how critical infrastructure stays secure.
Write like thisAutonomous fleets. Continuous coverage. No personnel required.
Not like thisOur AI-powered robotic solutions deliver 24/7 situational awareness across your entire facility footprint.
Part 3 — Information architecture

Page structure and section intent

01
Hero
One line: the problem space. One line: what SMR does. Two CTAs: "Request a briefing" + "See how it works." Ambient infrastructure video or dark static image.
02
Why now
Power demand is accelerating. Infrastructure is aging. Compliance requirements are tightening. The workforce can't scale to meet it. One paragraph, no numbers that could become wrong.
03
What the fleet does
Four pillars: Security · Inspection · Compliance support · Incident response. Each gets a single action-verb sentence. No capability claims that require certification to back up.
04
The model
Fleet as a managed service — deployed, operated, and maintained by SMR. No hardware purchase. No headcount. Outcome-based pricing discussed on a call, not on the page.
05
Built for regulated environments
Designed with critical infrastructure compliance frameworks in mind. Domestic-first hardware sourcing approach. On-premises data architecture. Auditable by design. No specific certification badges — directional language only.
06
Who we work with
Vertical logos or category labels only (utilities, data centers, C&I) — no named clients, no named partners until those relationships are public and confirmed.
07
Team + origin
High-security operations background → the problem became obvious → built the solution. Short. No internal strategy disclosed.
08
Two-track CTA
"I operate a site" → pilot inquiry. "I'm an investor or partner" → briefing request. Both go to a lightweight form or a direct email — not a generic contact page.
Part 4 — Safe copy rules

What to say vs. what to avoid

Compliance — say this

"Designed with NERC CIP and critical infrastructure compliance requirements in mind." "Built for regulated environments." "Architecture supports on-premises data and audit requirements."

Compliance — never say this

"NERC CIP compliant." "NDAA certified." Any specific standard as a completed claim rather than a design intent.

Hardware sourcing — say this

"Domestic-first hardware sourcing approach." "Supply chain built for enterprise security requirements." "Designed to meet government procurement standards."

Hardware sourcing — never say this

"No Chinese hardware." "100% domestic." Any absolute claim that current hardware mix can't fully support.

Pricing — say this

"Fleet-as-a-service model with outcome-based pricing." "Structured to replace multiple cost centers simultaneously." Pricing discussed in a briefing.

Pricing — never say this

Any specific number — dollar figures on a public site lock you in and signal cost structure to competitors.

Traction / scale — say this

"Deployed across energy infrastructure sites." "Operating in utility, data center, and industrial environments." Vague enough to be true at any deployment stage.

Traction / scale — never say this

Specific site counts, pipeline numbers, revenue figures — these belong to other entities and will change.

These 8 phrases are directionally accurate, strategically safe, and durable regardless of how the business evolves. Use them as the site's core vocabulary.
